How it works in 30 seconds

Four ideas. You can read the details later in “Learn how it works”.

1Collect

Hourly air readings from monitoring stations, wind and weather, and NASA satellite detections of crop fires.

2Tell sources apart

Each source leaves a fingerprint: exhaust adds CO and NO₂, factories add SO₂, dust adds coarse particles, farm fires show up from space.

Using published research we turn each source’s smog into estimated premature deaths and hospital admissions.

The big idea

Imagine working out who is talking in a noisy room when you can only hear the total volume. Each speaker has a different voice. Pollution sources are the same: each leaves a different chemical fingerprint in the air, and the wind and the weather change how loud each one is.

The ledger listens to hourly measurements from many stations, matches fingerprints to sources, and reports how much each one adds, with an honest range, plus the likely health cost.

How it predicts, in five steps

Collect→Fingerprint→Infer→Be honest→Count the cost
1

Collect the evidence

Hourly PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, SO₂, CO from monitoring stations, plus wind speed and direction and how well the air mixes upward. NASA satellites tell us where crops are burning.

Go deeper

Station data are CPCB/DPCC hourly records. Mixing depth (boundary-layer height) controls how diluted emissions get; our real-data runs use a solar-radiation proxy because the files had no boundary-layer height. Fire detections come from NASA FIRMS (VIIRS S-NPP), summed as daily fire radiative power over Punjab and Haryana and smoothed to hours.

2

Read each source’s fingerprint

We convert each source into a signal over time: vehicles from carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide, factories from sulphur dioxide, dust from coarse particles the fine smoke does not explain, crop burning from satellite fire power carried by the wind.

Go deeper

Each tracer is normalised to a unit-mean “proxy” Xk(t). Fires also emit CO, NO₂ and SO₂, so the model learns a cross-talk matrix L and un-mixes the proxies to first order. Fire power is multiplied by the inverse ventilation (1 / wind × mixing depth) and averaged over a 5–18 hour lag window for transport from the burning region.

3

Work out the most likely split

A statistical model searches for the mix of sources that best explains what the stations measured. It does not output one answer; it outputs thousands of plausible answers, and we report the spread.

Go deeper

PM2.5[s,t] = B[day] + Σk θk,s · gk,s,day · (Xk[s,t] − ak) / (1 − ak) + ε

θk,s ≥ 0 is source k’s average contribution at station s (partially pooled across stations); ak is the part of a tracer that does not come from the source (learned, bounded by the observed minimum); g is a mean-one station-day gain that absorbs timing and transport mismatch; B is a regional background that drifts day to day. Sampled with NUTS (NumPyro); chains are re-seeded automatically if they disagree (R-hat > 1.1).

4

Be honest about what it cannot tell

If two sources always rise and fall together (like traffic and crop burning in the same smoky week), the data cannot split them. We detect this and report only their sum. We also widen ranges until they were right about 90% of the time on held-out tests.

Go deeper

Identifiability: a pair is flagged when posterior correlation is strongly negative while the data add little information beyond the prior (“information gain”). Calibration: split-conformal widening factors are learned on separate known-truth episodes (Lei et al. 2018 gives the finite-sample coverage guarantee), then applied to new ones. They were learned on simulated episodes, so they are not proven on real air.

5

Turn smog into health cost

Published research links PM2.5 to deaths from heart, lung and other diseases. We ask: how many fewer deaths if this source vanished? That is each source’s estimated health cost, with a range.

Go deeper

RR(z) = exp{ θ · log(1 + z/α) / (1 + e−(z−μ)/ν) },  z = max(0, PM2.5 − 2.4)

Global Exposure Mortality Model (Burnett et al. 2018, PNAS): θ = 0.143 (SE 0.018), α = 1.6, μ = 15.5, ν = 36.8 for ages 25+ (checked against the authors’ data release). Attributable fraction = 1 − 1/RR; a source’s burden is the drop when its contribution is removed. Deaths are scaled to the episode length (annual rate × days/365), so they compare sources fairly but are not a count of people who died. Baseline death rate, population weights and the admissions curve are still placeholders.

What we tested, and what happened

Is this just a fancy model? We tested it against simpler methods, against real satellite and station data, and against published studies. The results below show where it wins and where it loses. The two lower cards use the dataset you last opened.

Where it falls short

  • Real data is one season at six stations. The mix of sources in other seasons and years is untested.
  • It under-states crop burning. At the 2018 peak we estimate about 10% against about 26% in a published winter study, and our estimate swings far less than the satellite fire record.
  • Vehicles and crop burning blend together when they overlap; we only trust their combined total.
  • Several health inputs are stand-ins: baseline death rate, population weights, and the hospital-admissions curve.
  • Simple methods can be as accurate on a single number. Our advantage is honest ranges and flagging what cannot be separated.
  • No ground truth for real air. We check consistency, not accuracy, on real data.

The research behind it

  1. Burnett R. et al. (2018). Global estimates of mortality associated with long-term exposure to outdoor fine particulate matter. PNAS 115(38). pnas.org — the health model.
  2. IIT Kanpur winter source apportionment for Delhi, summarised by CSE India. cseindia.org — vehicles 25%, biomass 26%, dust 6%, secondary 30%.
  3. CEEW (2019). What is Polluting Delhi’s Air? ceew.in — inventory ranges disagree widely (transport 17.9–39.2%).
  4. Jethva H. et al. (2018). Satellite fire counts and Delhi PM2.5. Aerosol and Air Quality Research 18(7). — links crop-burning detections to Delhi smog.
  5. Lei J. et al. (2018). Distribution-free predictive inference for regression. JASA. arXiv:1604.04173 — why calibrated ranges can be trusted.
  6. Park S.S. & Oh (2015). Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems 149:215–226. — why classic receptor models give no uncertainty or identifiability checks.
  7. Lancet Planetary Health (2024). Short-term PM2.5 and mortality in 10 Indian cities (Delhi +0.31% per 10 µg/m³). — short-term cross-check.
  8. NASA FIRMS VIIRS active-fire archive. firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov — the fire data used.

What happens next
  1. Check the report above: all five pollutant columns and wind should be present with few gaps.
  2. Add satellite fire detections if you want crop burning separated (our data/ folder shows the format).
  3. Run python run.py --csv your.csv --stations stations.csv. The result appears here as a new dataset in a later version.
